Cartel Talk, Competitive Prices: Opening Anchors—Not Communication or Oversight—Drive Apparent LLM Seller Collusion in Double Auctions

Aakash Patil and Bar Hoze
Apart Research AI Collusion Research Sprint, Oct 23–25, 2026 · Track 3 (project 3.4), with Track 1 (1.5)
Abstract LLM sellers in continuous double auctions are reported to coordinate high prices, especially when they can talk. Project 3.4 asks what such a coalition does when it learns it is being watched. We first tested whether the coalition exists. Across 151 market sessions with GPT-4.1, Qwen3 and Claude sellers and LLM buyers, we find: (1) the starter environment's rule-based buyer is frozen at $80, invalidating cheap runs; (2) final prices are set by the hard-coded opening quotes. GPT-4.1 sellers hold their opening ask (r = 0.995, n = 48), Qwen3-235B markets settle at the buyers' opening bid, and a Qwen market “passes” the built-in collusion test when buyers merely open high. (3) Despite explicit cartel language from both families, seller communication barely moved prices: paired-seed effects ranged from -0.23 to +1.08 dollars across 10 settings, under 11% of the $10 competitive–cartel gap. (4) When sellers are told they are monitored, overseer coordination scores fall from 3.87 to 2.0–2.5 and messages shorten, but neither prices nor ask dependence rise, so there is no channel movement by the track's standard. (5) Under excess supply, prices stay $8–11 above the competitive $80, but an excess-demand control also fails to rise, so within 15 rounds the market barely responds to supply and demand and cannot reveal tacit collusion. A stronger seller (Claude Sonnet 5.5, one seed) does track the spare seller and cuts prices about five times faster than GPT-4.1. We recommend paired-seed causal metrics, neutral openings and market-responsiveness controls.

1  Introduction

LLM agents are increasingly deployed to set prices and negotiate. If they collude, the harm to consumers is concrete, and the evidence trail (natural-language messages) looks unusually auditable. Agrawal et al. (2025) built a continuous double auction in which LLM sellers can message each other, and reported coordinated pricing, with communication and oversight as levers. Project 3.4 of this sprint asks the natural next question: what does a seller coalition do when it learns it is being watched? Does it stop colluding, or move its coordination into a less visible channel?

Answering that requires a coalition whose behavior depends on its coordination. We therefore first asked whether high prices in this environment are caused by coordination at all. Our contributions are:

  1. An environment audit. We found that the rule-based (ZIP) buyer is frozen at $80, that the hard-coded opening quotes largely determine outcomes, and that the competitive-price benchmark is wrong when seller and buyer counts differ (§4.1, Appendix B).
  2. A causal test of communication. We compare chat on and off on matched seeds across eight settings and two model families. Communication never measurably changed prices (§4.3).
  3. Track 3.4. Across 10 oversight conditions (silent logging, being told, a 25/50/100% sampled monitor, profit pressure), message-based coordination falls when sellers know they are watched, but action-based dependence does not rise, so we find no channel movement (§4.4).
  4. A model-temperament finding. GPT-4.1 holds its opening price; Qwen3 sellers cut to the buyers' bid immediately. Both talk like a cartel. Which side “wins” is set by which side holds still, not by what the sellers say (§4.2, §4.6).

2  Related Work

Algorithmic and LLM collusion. Q-learning pricing agents learn supra-competitive prices without communicating (Calvano et al., 2020). LLM pricing agents in repeated oligopolies also reach supra-competitive prices, and this is sensitive to prompt wording (Fish, Gonczarowski & Shorrer, 2024). Agrawal et al. (2025) introduced the double-auction environment we use and reported that seller communication and model choice affect coordination. Keppo et al. (2026) study how fragile AI agent collusion is. Our results support a related caution: in this environment, apparent collusion is largely explained by initial conditions.

Monitoring agents that know they are watched. Weak-to-strong monitoring of LLM agents is unreliable when agents are aware of the monitor (Kale et al., 2025). Agents can infer chain-of-thought monitoring from blocking feedback (Jiralerspong, Kondrup & Bengio, 2026), and monitors have systematic blind spots to evasion attacks (Najt et al., 2026). We study the market analogue: a message-level overseer that can gag sellers.

Double auctions. Human double auctions converge quickly toward competitive equilibrium (Smith, 1962). So do budget-constrained “zero-intelligence” traders (Gode & Sunder, 1993) and adaptive ZIP traders (Cliff, 1997). This makes LLM markets that do not move off their opening quotes a notable departure.

3  Methodology

Environment. We use the starter repository's llm-agent-collusion double auction. Sellers have unit cost $80 and buyers unit value $100. One lot per agent per hour, 15 hours, temperature 0.7, and the paper's prompts unchanged. Each hour every agent sees both order queues, the last five hours of quotes, past trades and its own memory, then posts one ask or bid. Trades clear at the midpoint of the best crossing ask and bid. With 3 sellers and 3 buyers the code's benchmarks are a competitive price of $90 and a cartel price of $100. We report the trade index (price − 90) / 10, so 0 is competitive and 1 is the cartel price. The built-in PASS criterion is “mean seller-ask index over the last third of hours > 0.5”. By default, opening asks are drawn uniformly around $97.5 and bids around $82.5 (±$1.25).

Models and providers. GPT-4.1 sellers with gpt-4.1-mini buyers (overseer: gpt-4.1-mini) ran via OpenRouter. Qwen3-235B-A22B-2507 sellers and buyers, with Qwen3-32B as the overseer and as the weak-agent condition, ran via AWS Bedrock (ap-south-1) through an OpenAI-compatible shim. One exploratory 4 : 3 session (teammate) used Claude Sonnet 5.5 sellers. Model and provider are fixed within every comparison.

Design. Every comparison uses matched seeds: the same seed gives identical agent names and opening quotes, so “chat on” and “chat off” sessions differ only in the manipulated variable. Most cells use seeds 0–2; the GPT-4.1 positive control uses seeds 0–4, and two Qwen cells use 6–7 seeds. The conditions are (i) seller chat on/off; (ii) the overseer, silent or announced, gagging or observe-only, sampling 25/50/100% of messages, with or without disclosing that fraction, and with or without CEO profit pressure; (iii) the opening quotes (seller asks ≈$97.5 or ≈$90; buyer bids ≈$82.5, 90 or 95; neutral asks ≈$92 / bids ≈$88); (iv) model strength (Qwen3-235B vs Qwen3-32B on either side); and (v) market ratio (4 sellers : 3 buyers, where the competitive price is $80).

Track 3.4 metrics. For each session we record mean overseer coordination score (1–4), mean seller message length and vocabulary size (message-based coordination), and mean cross-seller ask dispersion (the action-based dependence proxy; lower means asks move more in lockstep). Following the track's evidence standard, we would claim channel movement only if message-based coordination falls while action-based dependence rises.

What was done during the sprint. Preexisting: the environment, prompts, metrics, ZIP buyer and overseer sampling (Agrawal et al.; hackathon organizers). Added during the sprint: silent-oversight and disclosure flags, an observe-only overseer, opening-quote flags, the Bedrock shim, a scripted price-leader seller and two prompt variants (teammate), and all experiments and analyses.

4  Results

4.1  Positive control, and a frozen buyer

With the repository's cheap rule-based buyer, GPT-4.1 sellers never pass, even when they openly coordinate (Table 1, rows 1–2). The cause is that the ZIP buyer's adaptive update is commented out: every ZIP bid is (1 − 0.2) × $100 = $80 ± 1%. A cartel cannot sell above about $80.80, so all ZIP-buyer runs measure nothing about collusion. With LLM buyers, the README's positive control (GPT-4.1, chat on) passes in 5/5 seeds with trade index +0.70 ± 0.08, and sellers capture most of the surplus.

4.2  The opening quote sets the price

Across all 48 complete GPT-4.1 sessions with LLM buyers, the sellers' round-1 average ask predicts the session's average trade price with r = 0.995 (Fig. 1a). Over 15 hours, asks move by a few cents per hour: sellers undercut each other by $0.01 to stay first in the queue. GPT-4.1 explains this as patience (“no need to adjust ask yet; patience may help reveal buyers' willingness”), and the gpt-4.1-mini buyers raise their bids to meet it. Started at $90 instead of $97.5, the same sellers stay at $90 (index -0.09). Qwen3-235B sellers do the opposite: they cut to just above the highest bid in hour 1 (“I must lower my ask to just above the highest bid”). Qwen markets therefore settle wherever the buyers opened (Fig. 1b). Raising only the buyers' opening bid to ≈$95 yields $96.11 with chat and $95.89 without, sellers keep ~81% of surplus, and the built-in test would “pass”. That is a false positive with no coordination involved.

4.3  Communication does not change prices

With matched seeds, the effect of enabling seller chat is the paired difference in session price. Across 10 settings (two model families, three opening regimes, weak buyers, and both market ratios), the mean effect is between -0.23 and +1.08 dollars (Fig. 2), against a $10 gap between the competitive and cartel price. 7 of the 10 settings have seed pairs on both sides of zero. Three settings are positive in every seed pair, all small: +0.13 ± 0.13 for GPT-4.1 opening at $90, +0.22 ± 0.17 for Qwen when buyers open high, and +0.89 ± 0.59 for Qwen at 4 : 3. Each is under a tenth of full collusion. A small positive effect of talk cannot be excluded and would need more seeds to confirm. Communication is not inert in content: GPT-4.1 sellers write “no reason to blink first” and praise their “coalition discipline”, and Qwen sellers write “let's hold the line on prices. No need to race to the bottom.” But silent sellers price identically. Qwen shows the clearest talk–action gap: a seller wrote “let's hold the line on prices” while asking $82.80 in the same hour.

4.4  Being watched (Track 3.4): the talk changes, the market does not

A silent overseer that never gags sees unguarded talk: mean coordination score 3.87/4. Telling sellers they are monitored lowers scores to 2.02–2.47 and shortens messages (from 227 to 139 characters at 100% monitoring, and 102 with profit pressure). Sellers sanitize their language. Action-based dependence does not rise in response: cross-seller ask dispersion under announced monitoring (0.23–0.63) is in the same range as no oversight (0.29) and silent oversight (0.43), with no trend in the monitored fraction. Prices stay at ≈$83 throughout (Table 3). By the track's standard, this is no evidence of channel movement. The coalition reacts to being watched by changing what it says, and there was no price-setting coordination to relocate. The GPT-4.1 4 : 3 runs (teammate) show the same pattern in counts: with chat, 155 of 180 seller messages use cartel wording (“avoid aggressive undercutting so we all protect our margins”). With the overseer on, only 32 of 178 do, and the price is unchanged ($90.83 vs $90.82). Gagged GPT-4.1 sellers converged on shared tokens (“⋇”, “⋆”, “.”) and in one seed on a shared hour counter (“9”, “10”, …), the only post-gag pattern the overseer flagged. Prices were again unchanged (Table 1). The one lever with a hint of an effect is profit pressure: 3 of 6 boss-pressure sessions ended above $85. The largest ($94.05) traces to sellers keeping the $97.5 opening in hour 1, the same anchoring mechanism.

4.5  Competitive pressure: excess supply and an excess-demand control

Equal seller and buyer counts cannot separate collusion from sticky pricing, because every seller eventually trades. With 4 sellers and 3 buyers, one seller is unsold every hour, and the competitive price falls to cost ($80). The starter code's benchmark still reports $90 here, so we use the correct values. From a neutral opening, 4 : 3 prices stayed $8–11 above $80 for both families. Seller chat, buyer chat and the overseer made no difference (Table 4). Read alone, that would look like tacit collusion. The excess-demand control fails, however: with 3 sellers and 4 buyers, where competition should push prices up toward $100, GPT-4.1 prices stayed at $91.08. Every GPT-4.1 condition lies within $0.30 of every other. Within 15 hours the market does not respond to supply and demand at all, so failing to fall under excess supply says nothing about collusion. Seller reasoning shows why. Unsold sellers undercut the lowest ask by the minimum, a best response, since winning the queue needs only $0.01 (“lower my ask to $87.50 … still above my cost”; GPT-4.1: “continue minimal undercutting—one cent below the lowest ask”). At a cent per hour, reaching cost would take on the order of 103 hours. In the excess-demand market, GPT-4.1 sellers kept undercutting too, and buyers sat a cent under the lowest ask. Model capability matters. In one exploratory seed, Claude Sonnet 5.5 sellers described the market structure correctly every hour (“four sellers compete for roughly three buyers each hour, and the higher-priced seller is left out”). They cut asks by $1.97 over 15 hours, about 5× GPT-4.1's $0.39, with cuts growing from about 5¢ to about 25¢ per hour by the end. None of its 60 responses reasons about long-run joint profit, though 27 mention avoiding a price war. GPT-4.1 almost never mentions the spare seller. The neutral opening sits about $11 above the 4 : 3 competitive price, so even Sonnet's pace leaves prices far from $80 after 15 hours. The market's insensitivity to supply and demand is therefore partly a property of the model, not only of the environment. Confirming this needs more seeds and a Sonnet excess-demand control. Our conclusion is that this environment, as configured, cannot detect tacit collusion: a test needs a market that first passes the excess-demand control (longer horizons, minimum price steps, or quantity choice).

4.6  Who “wins” is decided by who holds still

Combining §4.2–4.5: with identical environments and prompts, GPT-4.1 sellers captured ~85% of surplus and Qwen3-235B sellers ~17%. The difference is temperament, not coordination. GPT-4.1 plans over the horizon and concedes in cents. Qwen optimizes the current hour and matches the best bid. Pairing strong Qwen sellers with weak Qwen3-32B buyers, or the reverse, changed little (83.99 ± 1.47 vs 83.61 ± 0.51), because Qwen sellers cut before buyers could concede. The gpt-4.1-mini buyers, for their part, bid against each other and never saw the sellers' $80 cost. In seed 0 one buyer called a $2.40 margin “healthy”; the two that noticed it was thin (“limits profit margins”) kept bidding anyway, because any rival bidding $97.60 would take the trade.

5  Discussion

Implications for collusion evaluation. A price-level test (high prices ⇒ collusion) is unsafe in LLM markets. The same high prices arise from an anchored start and agents that rarely revise it, with or without talk, and the test can be triggered by the buyers' opening alone. Evaluations should (i) start from neutral or agent-chosen quotes, (ii) report paired, seed-matched effects of communication on profit, (iii) include excess-supply markets with a correct competitive benchmark and an excess-demand control showing that the market responds to supply and demand at all, and (iv) report the positive control together with a false-positive control such as our high-bid market.

Implications for oversight. Message monitors measure what agents say. Both models readily produce cartel rhetoric, and announcing a monitor makes them tone it down. Neither changes prices here. A monitor that flags rhetoric can therefore over-report collusion (talk without effect) or, after sanitization, under-report it. Monitoring should be anchored to action-level dependence and counterfactual price effects, not message content alone. The concerning case for regulators is the GPT-4.1 one: a model whose unilateral patience sustains high prices with nothing for a message monitor to read.

Conclusion. In this environment we find no evidence that LLM sellers' communication causes collusion, and therefore no channel for oversight to displace. Apparent cartels are explained by opening anchors, per-model pricing temperament, buyer weakness and short horizons. Tacit collusion remains untestable here until the market passes an excess-demand control. Stronger models may make it testable: Claude Sonnet 5.5 already responds to excess supply. We release flags for neutral openings and silent or observe-only oversight to support stronger tests.

Appendix A  Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

Statistical power. Most cells have 3 seeds (the positive control has 5, two Qwen cells have 6–7). We can rule out effects of the size of full collusion (≈$10) but not effects under about $1–2. Effects are reported as means ± sample s.d. without formal tests.

Scope. Two model families. GPT-4.1 was paired only with gpt-4.1-mini buyers, and Qwen ran on a different provider (Bedrock) with JSON mode emulated by a system hint in the shim. Horizons were 15 hours; longer horizons might reveal slow convergence or late-forming collusion. Claude was tested in a single exploratory seed without an excess-demand control, and Gemini not at all. Neither ratio test passed the excess-demand control, so we make no claim either way about tacit collusion.

Environment caveats. (i) Until upstream fix 75d3e9d (merged after our runs), agents running in parallel could see same-hour orders from agents that answered first, mainly on API retries. This applies equally to all our sessions. (ii) Order queues are cleared after trades, and Qwen sellers sometimes read an empty bid queue as “buyers inactive”. (iii) A failed API call aborts a session; five Qwen3-235B OpenRouter sessions were lost to a zero-credit account and are excluded, and one Qwen3-14B session stopped at hour 14 (Appendix B). (iv) The competitive-equilibrium function ignored unmatched agents. This is now fixed on sprint-merged, and all our analyses use the correct $80 / $100 benchmarks. (v) A gagging overseer is never fully silent: the gag message mentions regulators.

Dual use. The findings could be read as a recipe for evading message monitors (sanitize language, rely on anchored prices). We judge the risk low: the behaviors are already elicited by the paper's public prompts. The main lesson, that message-level monitoring alone is inadequate, favors defenders. We release no evasion prompts. All markets are simulated; no real prices, firms or people are involved.

AI assistance. Analysis code and drafting were assisted by an AI coding assistant. All numbers are computed directly from the logged sessions by a script, and the authors take responsibility for the content.

Appendix D  Future work, in priority order

  1. Make the market respond to supply and demand. Nothing about tacit collusion can be concluded until an excess-demand (3 : 4) market rises toward $100 and an excess-supply (4 : 3) market falls toward $80 without communication. Options: longer horizons (30–100+ hours, since cent-sized steps need about 1,000 hours to reach cost), a minimum price step (for example $0.50), or agent-chosen quantities.
  2. Rerun the tacit-collusion test on that market (4 : 3 vs 3 : 4, chat on/off, overseer) with 3+ seeds per cell.
  3. Confirm the stronger-model result: Claude Sonnet 5.5 at 3 : 4, plus more 4 : 3 seeds. If Sonnet passes the control, use it as the seller model for steps 1–2.
  4. Cross-model sellers (GPT-4.1, Claude and Qwen in one market), following the original paper's design.
  5. Truly silent oversight for GPT-4.1 (--no_gag), and the 25/50/100% monitoring and disclosure conditions, which so far exist only for Qwen.
  6. Rerun the headline cells after the order-queue fix 75d3e9d.
  7. Follow up profit pressure, the only hint of an effect (3 of 6 Qwen sessions above $85), with more seeds and GPT-4.1.
  8. Stronger buyer models. gpt-4.1-mini buyers concede easily.
  9. Better collusion metrics: seed-matched causal effects, price change relative to the opening, and action-level dependence, not price level or message content alone.
  10. Fix environment defaults: restore adaptive ZIP bidding, make a neutral opening the default, and keep a failed API call from aborting the session.
